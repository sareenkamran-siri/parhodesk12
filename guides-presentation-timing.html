WRITING

Turn a script into a realistic presentation plan

ParhoDesk guide · Updated October 1, 2026

Words are only part of the time

A speaking-time estimate divides script words by words per minute. At 130 words per minute, a 650-word script takes five minutes to say continuously. A real presentation also includes slide changes, breathing, emphasis, demonstrations, and perhaps questions. Treat the calculated time as a starting point, then reserve time for those activities.

Find your own pace

Choose a representative passage rather than the easiest paragraph. Read it aloud naturally for one minute and count the words you actually spoke. Repeat with a technical section. If one trial gives 145 words and another gives 115, try planning around the slower pace. A presentation is not a race; unfamiliar terms and equations deserve time.

Build a time budget

For a five-minute talk, you might reserve 30 seconds for opening, three minutes for your main explanation, one minute for an example, and 30 seconds for a conclusion. If questions count toward the limit, reserve them explicitly. Make the main message clear enough that removing an optional example will not make the rest confusing.

Rehearse with the real materials

Stand up, show the slides, and perform any demonstration. Start a timer before your first words and stop after your final sentence. Mark the time at major transitions. If you consistently run long, shorten the script instead of speeding up every sentence. Keep important definitions, evidence, and transitions; trim repeated setup and secondary anecdotes.

Use pauses deliberately

Place a pause after the main claim and before a complex result. Short silences help listeners process information. A word counter cannot detect these pauses, so add them during rehearsal. When reading a quote, slow down and identify the source. When showing a diagram, let the audience locate the feature before you discuss it.

Prepare a shorter ending

Have a concise closing sentence ready in case you reach the time limit early. It should restate the main takeaway without introducing a new argument. Do not rely on a last-minute speed increase: it can make your most important point the hardest to follow. Your own recorded rehearsal is a better final check than any generic pace estimate.
